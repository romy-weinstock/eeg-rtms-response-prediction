# Modelling decisions: primary-arm pool, classifiers, and evaluation design

This document records the modelling decisions for this project, following the same discipline used throughout preprocessing and extraction: each decision is stated with its reasoning and its sources, distinguishing what's grounded in a specific citation from what's a project-level judgment call.

## 1. Primary dataset variant: heog_off

VEOG correction is validated to a high standard: isolated blink-shaped segments, quiet baseline, expected frontal-to-posterior contamination gradient. HEOG correction remains uncertain despite genuine effort (duration-bound tightening, literature-grounded bounds, window-size-sweep-validated drift removal): roughly half of detected segments show step-shift/trend character rather than isolated events, and a known implementation gap means regression would run against non-detrended HEOG data, inconsistent with the detrended data used for detection. Applying an uncertain correction risks actively subtracting real EEG signal using misidentified noise as the regressor; leaving it uncorrected only retains ordinary,  contamination. Not correcting was judged the lower-risk default, but not a reason to drop the question: the full cohort was run twice (heog_off, heog_on), so the on/off comparison can be tested empirically rather than assumed. heog_off is primary; heog_on is the dedicated sensitivity-check arm.

## 2. Frequency bands, connectivity metric(s), and power transform

**Bands.** No single authoritative band-boundary table exists in the EEG literature. Newson and Thiagarajan's (2019) review of resting-state EEG studies in psychiatric research exists specifically because definitions vary substantially across the field, and reported ranges commonly span delta 1 to 4 Hz, theta 4 to 8 Hz, alpha 8 to 13 Hz, beta 13 to 30 Hz, with gamma anywhere from 30 to 45 Hz to 30 to 100 Hz depending on the study. Given no standard to defer to, bands were matched to Chang et al. (2025): delta 2 to 4 Hz, theta 4 to 8 Hz, alpha 8 to 13 Hz, beta 13 to 30 Hz, gamma 30 to 45 Hz, the closest direct comparator, since it addresses the same prediction problem (pre-treatment resting EEG predicting rTMS response in MDD) on a comparable sample size (n=94 after exclusions). This is a judgment call justified by comparability to the nearest published benchmark, not a claim that this table is the field standard.

**Connectivity metric.** PLI is primary, with coherence and PLV as secondary comparisons. This is grounded in a specific mechanism, not convention: Stam, Nolte, and Daffertshofer (2007) introduced PLI to address volume conduction and reference-electrode bias in EEG connectivity estimation, and found it performed at least as well as conventional phase coherence at detecting genuine synchronization changes while being substantially less affected by common-source contamination. The mechanism is that PLI and the imaginary part of coherency both discard zero-lag phase relationships, on the reasoning that instantaneous (zero-lag) synchrony between electrodes is more likely to reflect the same underlying source picked up at two sensors than genuine inter-regional coupling, whereas plain coherence and standard PLV can be artificially inflated between spatially close electrodes for exactly this reason. Running coherence/PLV as secondary comparisons alongside PLI gives a direct empirical check on how much of any connectivity-based predictive signal survives volume-conduction correction, mirroring the heog_off/heog_on sensitivity-check structure already used for preprocessing. Caveat for the record: PLI has been reported to have lower test-retest reliability than some PLV variants, so volume-conduction robustness and measurement reliability are separate properties.

**Power transform.** Band power and ratio power features are log-transformed; PLI, PLV, and coherence features are not. The reason for the split: log-normality evidence (Box-Cox optimization studies on resting alpha power, and general findings on EEG power-spectrum skewness) applies to unbounded, right-skewed spectral power. It doesn't apply to PLI/PLV/coherence, which are bounded between 0 and 1 and don't share that skewed shape. Log-transforming a bounded [0,1] metric is also risky in practice, since values near zero blow up toward negative infinity, and these connectivity metrics can legitimately sit near zero for unconnected channel pairs. The benefit of the transform also depends on the model: LDA assumes normally-distributed features within each class, so log power directly fixes a violated assumption there. Regularized linear models (logistic regression, linear SVM) benefit less formally, but still gain more even-handed regularization once features are on comparable scales. Tree ensembles are unaffected either way, since they're invariant to monotonic transforms. As with the planned age-confound regression, this transform will be fit on training folds only at the modelling stage, not applied to the full feature matrix beforehand. (Exception: FAA - see Decision 5.)

**PSD method**: Welch's method (method='welch' in MNE's compute_psd). Chang et al. (2025) states "FFT" without specifying segmentation, which is ambiguous — Roelofs et al. (2021)'s comparably-described "FFT" approach (segmented, overlapping, Hamming-windowed) is Welch's method by construction despite the label, and Stolz et al. (2023), a separate TDBRAIN-based rTMS response prediction study, explicitly names Welch. Welch is treated as the best-supported reading of "FFT-based PSD" across this literature.

## 3. Epoch-to-subject aggregation and minimum epoch-count threshold

Subjects are aggregated by simple mean across retained epochs, per feature, per subject. This is supported unevenly across feature types rather than uniformly. Dominicus et al. (2025) assessed how strongly three classes of resting-state EEG metrics, functional connectivity (amplitude-envelope correlation and PLI), signal complexity (permutation entropy, PE), and network topology (minimum spanning trees), depend on time and state, and found that power spectral characteristics show high temporal stability, while functional connectivity measures are considerably more variable, with stability decreasing further as retest interval increases from hours to days to a month or longer. The same study found alpha and theta PE, and alpha PLI specifically, to be the most robust measures suitable for biomarker development, with other connectivity metrics requiring caution due to limited stability. This is a second, independent line of evidence supporting PLI as the primary connectivity metric (Decision 2), beyond its volume-conduction robustness. Plain mean aggregation is treated as an adequate default for power features and a known limitation for connectivity features, documented as such rather than resolved with a different aggregation scheme, since Dominicus et al. (2025) do not propose or test a superior alternative for this case.

The minimum epoch-count inclusion threshold is set at approximately 10 epochs (50 seconds of retained resting-state data), anchored to Wiesman et al. (2022), who found that spectral estimate stability in resting-state MEG can be robustly assessed from segments of roughly 30 to 120 seconds, depending on cortical region. Strijbis et al. (2022), citing Wiesman et al., adopted a working floor of 5 epochs of 13 seconds (65 seconds total) on that basis. Translated to this project's 5-second epochs, the Wiesman et al. range corresponds to roughly 6 to 24 epochs. This transfer is treated explicitly as an approximation, not a precise cutoff: Wiesman et al.'s evidence is MEG source-level spectral stability, not EEG sensor-level connectivity, so it anchors the floor rather than proving it.

Retained-epoch-count was checked for correlation with age (n=160, restEC/heog_off, excluding the 3 subjects with missing BDF files): Spearman rho = -0.079 (p = 0.322), Pearson r = -0.116 (p = 0.145). Neither is significant. The floor does not introduce an age-related selection effect on top of the already-documented age confound (Decision 7).

In practice, the floor is non-binding for this cohort: retained epochs range from 21 to 24, well above the 10-epoch threshold, with no subjects falling below 15. The floor remains a necessary methodological decision to state and justify, but no subjects in this cohort are actually excluded or marginal because of it.

## 4. Resting condition: restEC primary, restEO as sensitivity arm

restEC is primary; restEO is the documented comparison arm, structured the same way as the heog_off/heog_on split (Decision 1).

No literature consensus favors either condition for this specific prediction task. Duan et al. (2021) found slightly higher general reproducibility for eyes-closed rsEEG. Alpha-band signal, relevant given the alpha-based biomarkers in the candidate feature literature, is substantially attenuated in eyes-open recordings (van Dijk et al., 2022, d = 0.89).

The pilot preprocessing notebook raised a concrete concern: one subject had zero valid VEOG segments in restEC, risking uncalibrated ocular correction if this generalized. Checked against the 6-subject batch test: it did not generalize. Zero subjects had zero valid VEOG segments in either condition, and restEC in fact produced more candidate and valid VEOG segments on average than restEO (42.5 vs. 27.8 candidates; 37.8 vs. 25.8 valid). Epoch retention was identical across conditions (median 0.958). HEOG candidate counts were lower and exclusion rates higher in restEC, but this doesn't affect the primary pipeline since HEOG correction is off in heog_off.

## 4a. Extraction scope: why these four feature families

Band power, PLI/coherence/PLV, and Kuramoto order parameter/metastability were extracted as three distinct signal properties: spectral power (how much oscillatory activity, per band, per channel), phase-synchrony between regions (how coupled two channels' oscillations are), and macroscopic phase-coupling dynamics (how synchronized the whole 26-channel system is, moment to moment). Each has independent justification, stated here on its own terms rather than as a byproduct of the connectivity- or Kuramoto-specific reasoning in Decisions 2 and 6.

**Band power** is the most extensively studied EEG feature type in this literature by a wide margin — the majority of the 81 biomarkers in Widge et al.'s (2019) meta-analysis are power-based, spanning many different bands, channels, and derived indices, giving band power the broadest evidentiary base of any feature type considered for this project. Two specific band-power-adjacent constructs surfaced during the literature search, individual alpha frequency and task-elicited frontal-midline theta power, are addressed separately in Decision 5 rather than counted here as direct support: the former is already the basis of this project's IAF-proximity supplementary analysis, and the latter cannot be computed from this project's resting-state data. Band power's justification for extraction rests on its breadth as a studied feature type, not on either of those two constructs individually.

**PLI, coherence, and PLV** are justified in full in Decision 2 (Stam, Nolte, & Daffertshofer, 2007; Dominicus et al., 2025) and not restated here.

**Kuramoto order parameter and metastability** are justified in full in Decision 6 (Escrichs et al., 2024; Bapat et al., 2024) and not restated here.

**Adjacent feature types identified but not extracted.** Three further feature types surfaced during the literature search with real supporting evidence, but weren't pursued: cordance (Leuchter et al., 1994; Widge et al.'s "closest to proof of concept" marker, with their own caveat that no marker beat the pooled estimate), the aperiodic 1/f exponent (outperformed band power on this exact task in a same-dataset analysis — Stolz et al., 2023), and permutation entropy (found comparably robust to alpha PLI — Dominicus et al., 2025, already cited in Decision 3). Each represents a different signal property than this project's three families (perfusion-linked power asymmetry, non-oscillatory background activity, and complexity, respectively).

Task-elicited frontal-midline theta power (Li et al., 2016, 2021; Pizzagalli et al., 2018; certified robust by Klooster et al., 2024) is excluded for a different, structural reason: it requires a cognitive task during EEG recording, which this project's resting-state design (Decision 4) doesn't include.

## 5. Classifier family, primary-arm pool, and dimensionality strategy

Four classifiers are compared across every arm: Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage LDA, elastic-net regularized logistic regression, Bayesian logistic regression with a regularizing prior, and unregularized logistic regression.

The exclusion of tree ensembles from this set is grounded in a direct comparator, not a general small-sample worry. Chang et al. (2025), working on the same prediction problem, found XGBoost's cross-validated accuracy fell from 84.11% to a mean of 63.79% (SD 4.61%) across 1,000 shuffles at n=75, while LDA held stable at 86.36%. Varoquaux (2018) explains why this instability is expected at these sample sizes: cross-validation error bars are substantially underestimated relative to their true variability once sample size drops into this range. XGBoost and random forest are tested only in the full feature-bank arm, specifically to check whether nonlinear flexibility captures signal the linear/shrinkage models miss; instability there, consistent with the Chang et al. finding, is an expected and reportable outcome, not a failure to avoid.

Standard LDA's requirement to invert a feature covariance matrix breaks down once feature count approaches sample count. Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004) addresses this directly, and Lotfi, Shahsavani, and Arashi (2022) found it outperforms standard LDA specifically in high-dimensional classification settings comparable to this project's feature-to-sample ratio. Bayesian logistic regression with a regularizing prior performs the same shrinkage function as elastic-net's penalty term, making it a small-sample-appropriate method by design.

**Unregularized logistic regression** is a fourth model reported in every arm, not a fourth candidate under the same justification as the other three — it is a diagnostic control, testing whether the other three models' regularization is doing measurable work at each arm's feature-to-sample ratio. At low ratios (Arm 1, p=1) it should perform comparably to the shrinkage models, since neither Ledoit-Wolf's shrinkage intensity nor elastic-net's penalty should do much work when p≪n. At high ratios (Arm 4) it should degrade, giving a direct, computed illustration of the instability Chang et al. (2025) report, rather than only a citation of it.

**Note.** Band power was never entered into the primary-arm pool as a specific construct. Widge et al.'s (2019) meta-analysis supports band power's extraction as a family (Decision 4a) but doesn't identify any particular channel/band combination as predictive, and no other source surveyed does either. Selecting a subset now could only be done by looking at which columns associate with responder status in this cohort's own labels — the same leakage risk that excludes Stolz et al. (2023) and Arteaga et al. (2025) as feature sources below, despite being the closest comparators. Band power is carried forward only in the full feature-bank arm, where nested feature selection inside cross-validation is the pre-specified mechanism for this kind of undirected search.

**Selection criteria for the primary-arm pool: a two-stage structure.**

*Stage 1 (eligibility) — independent-sample evidence only.* A construct qualifies only if its supporting evidence comes from a sample that doesn't overlap with TDBRAIN. This is a binary gate: using a same-dataset finding to justify a feature would mean selecting on association with the outcome in the data the model is later evaluated on — the leakage problem Shim, Lee, and Hwang (2021) document. This excludes Stolz et al. (2023) and Arteaga et al. (2025) as feature sources despite being the closest comparators in task and population — both are TDBRAIN secondary analyses with plausible subject overlap. Both remain cited as engineering-relevance context (Decision 4a), not as primary-pool justification.

*Stage 2 (weighting, among eligible candidates) — replication consistency, mechanistic specificity, translational actionability.* Passing Stage 1 does not require a positive result; it requires only that independent-sample evidence exists and gets reported honestly regardless of direction. Widge et al. (2019) found no single QEEG biomarker beat the pooled meta-analytic estimate across 81 biomarkers — retained as the honest baseline this pool is weighed against, not a reason to abandon literature-grounded selection (that would collapse the two-arm design into two sizes of the same approach, trading a thin-but-transparent literature base for an equally-constrained n=160 data-driven search; Varoquaux, 2018). Klooster et al. (2024), applying a strict robustness bar (N≥88 or independent replication) to 135 candidate EEG studies, certifies only two biomarkers as robust: individual alpha frequency and task-elicited frontal-midline theta power. Neither adds a primary-arm feature: individual alpha frequency is the Corlier (2019)/Roelofs (2021) pairing already driving the IAF-proximity supplementary analysis; task-elicited frontal-midline theta power can't be computed from this project's resting-state design (Decision 4a). Both are documented as evidence considered, not contributions to the feature list.

**Primary-arm pool: frontal alpha asymmetry (FAA), the sole construct.** FAA = alpha power(F4) − alpha power(F3), raw difference, matching Provaznikova et al. (2025) exactly rather than the normalized (F4−F3)/(F4+F3) variant also used elsewhere in the literature: Provaznikova is the independent-sample study specific to this task (TBS-rTMS response, n=46) that produced the cited result (responders showed significantly lower FAA; AUC=0.75–0.81, LOOCV-validated), so the formula that produced that result is the one being tested. Computed from untransformed alpha power at F3/F4 — an explicit exception to Decision 2's default log-transform, since FAA is a signed difference and log is undefined for negative values.

Contested, not simply supported: van der Vinne et al.'s (2017) meta-analysis addressed FAA as a *diagnostic* marker and found it unreliable; Provaznikova's independent-sample, *treatment-response*-specific finding is the positive evidence retained here — a different question, not a direct contradiction. FAA is not one of Klooster et al.'s (2024) two certified-robust biomarkers; the primary pool is smaller and less certain than Klooster's own bar, stated here rather than left implicit.

A bounded search for additional Stage-1-eligible constructs published since Klooster et al.'s (2024) review (2023 onward, criteria set before searching, matching Klooster's own N≥88-or-replication bar) found two candidates, neither qualifying: a proprietary multi-feature biomarker report (no specific citable construct — same problem as band power) and a dlPFC sensitivity index requiring TMS-evoked EEG (incompatible with this project's resting-state-only design). No construct was added as a result.

**Bailey construct: excluded from the primary pool, retained as a supplementary replication check.** Bailey et al. (2019) found theta (4–8 Hz) connectivity elevated in responders, via a network of electrode pairs derived post hoc (network-based statistics) from their own sample; alpha and theta power did not differ between groups in that study. Bailey et al. (2021), an independent multi-site replication (ICON-DB consortium, N≈193), tested the same measures — wPLI theta connectivity averaged across the 14 electrode pairs that overlapped between the original network and the replication montage, plus alpha power — and found no significant difference for either measure. A well-specified construct that has already failed independent, adequately-powered replication doesn't belong in a blended multivariate primary-pool model, but the question of whether it holds in a third independent sample is legitimate to report either way, so it is retained as its own pre-specified test, structured like IAF-proximity.

*Implementation.* Theta-band PLI (already extracted) as a documented substitute for wPLI, averaged across the 14 named pairs: Fz-FC4, FC3-FC4, F3-P3, FC3-P3, P3-P4, F3-O1, FC3-O1, P3-O1, P4-O1, F3-O2, FC3-O2, P3-O2, P4-O2, O1-O2 (all electrodes confirmed present in TDBRAIN's 26-channel montage). Alpha power (F3, F4, O1, O2) and theta power (Fz), already extracted, unchanged from Decision 4a. Test structure matches IAF-proximity: logistic regression (feature + age) primary, Mann-Whitney U companion.

**IAF-proximity** is excluded from the primary pool despite being the best-replicated single candidate (Corlier 2019; replicated by Roelofs 2021; certified by Klooster 2024), because its evidence covers only the 10Hz unilateral left-DLPFC subgroup — protocol 1, n=42/163 (26%). Extending it cohort-wide would leave 74% of subjects with no literature basis for the feature.

Instead, IAF-proximity is a dedicated supplementary analysis restricted to the n=42 protocol-1 subjects, structured like the heog_off/heog_on and restEC/restEO arms (Decisions 1, 4): pre-specified, reported regardless of outcome, not folded into the primary feature set. At n=42, this tests the same question Corlier and Roelofs asked — is IAF-proximity associated with responder status — via a direct association test, not a trained classifier.

Note on independence: Roelofs et al.'s (2021) replication sample comes from an open-label study (Donse et al., 2018; Krepel et al., 2019) at the same clinical program that contributed to TDBRAIN, though their paper doesn't cite TDBRAIN directly. Individual-subject overlap with this cohort's n=163 is unconfirmed, not ruled out — treated as a plausibly-independent check, not a confirmed one.

**IAF-proximity implementation addendum.** Alpha search window for peak-picking set to 7-13 Hz, matching Roelofs et al. (2021) exactly, as a deliberate exception to this project's primary 8-13 Hz alpha band (Decision 2) — justified by this feature's purpose being replication fidelity specifically. Electrode: F3, matching the 10 Hz left-DLPFC stimulation site per Roelofs et al. Low-alpha exclusion: checked and not applied — this project's n=42 sample shows no bimodal peak-power distribution resembling Roelofs et al.'s, so the empirical basis for their exclusion criterion is absent; all 42 subjects retained. Protocol-1 subgroup split: 17 non-responders, 25 responders — close to but slightly under the conventional 10-events-per-predictor floor for a two-predictor logistic regression, stated here as an explicit precision caveat.

**Protocol 3 discrepancy (unresolved).** van Dijk et al. (2022), Table 2, reports only 7 MDD subjects on rTMS protocol 3 across the full 1274-subject database. This cohort's V3.1 release shows 32 subjects coded protocol 3 (of 163) — confirmed not a denominator artefact (all 163 subjects have complete BDI pre/post; filtering to BDI-complete subjects does not change the count). The cause is unresolved: possibly dataset growth or recoding between the 2022 publication and the V3.1 release, or a difference in what the `rTMS PROTOCOL` field represents between versions. Flagged as an open item, and a candidate for direct follow-up with Brainclinics if it becomes material to the modelling results.

The full feature bank (`full_cohort_features.parquet`, 160×5031: band power, PLI, coherence, PLV, and Kuramoto order parameter/metastability across all channels and all five bands — confirmed complete and self-consistent at the matrix-assembly QC pass; see `feature_extraction_notes.md`) is used in the secondary, exploratory arm, with nested feature selection inside CV per Shim, Lee, and Hwang (2021).

**Full pre-specified evaluation set.** Six arms plus two supplementary tests, specified together and reported in full regardless of outcome, avoiding an implicit multiple-comparisons problem where only better-performing results get reported:

1. Primary pool alone (FAA)
2. Primary pool + Kuramoto (Decision 6)
3. PLI vs. coherence/PLV (Decision 2)
4. Full feature bank, nested selection, plus XGBoost/random forest
5. heog_off vs. heog_on (Decision 1)
6. restEC vs. restEO (Decision 4)

Supplementary: IAF-proximity association test (above); Bailey construct replication check (above).

## 6. Kuramoto synchrony metrics

Order parameter and metastability, computed per epoch using the same aggregation approach as the spectral and connectivity features, as a secondary comparison against the spectral/connectivity baseline — Arm 2 in the full pre-specified evaluation set (Decision 5): primary pool (FAA) alone vs. primary pool + Kuramoto, same four classifiers. This matches the project's stated framing: does synchrony add predictive value beyond standard features, not synchrony as a primary claim on its own. Full coupling-matrix fitting remains explicitly out of scope, given sample size constraints.

**Channel grouping: global (all 26 channels), not sub-grouped.** Three candidate grouping approaches were identified in the literature, none of which transposes to this project's data without introducing an ungrounded methodological step. Bapat, Pathak, and Banerjee (2024) derive channel groups from the TMS-evoked potential itself (sorting channels by deviation from the mean at the timepoint of peak Global Field Power) - inapplicable here, since there is no evoked stimulus in resting-state pre-treatment recordings to derive a grouping from. Escrichs et al. (2024) use a continuous spatial distance-decay kernel across a 1000-region fMRI parcellation - inapplicable given this project's 26-electrode spatial resolution. Schmidt et al. (2015) group nodes by predefined anatomical/functional modules and by structural rich-club/hub membership, but this is a simulation study on a 219-node DWI-derived connectome, not empirical EEG; mapping its module or hub-selection logic onto scalp electrodes would require a new methodological justification this project does not yet have. Given no grounded grouping method transfers, global whole-cohort KOP is computed as the primary Kuramoto feature; regional subgrouping is deferred as an unvalidated exploratory extension for the secondary arm, consistent with how IAF-proximity's limited evidence base was scoped down rather than extended past its support (Decision 5).

**Frequency bands: all five, with no assumed alpha primacy.** Scope follows the same completeness logic used for PLI and coherence/PLV (Decision 2). Unlike PLI, alpha does not carry a specific stability or mechanistic grounding for Kuramoto/metastability in the literature reviewed here — if anything, the one directly relevant EEG finding points away from alpha: Bapat et al. (2024) report the TMS-pulse-related decrease in the Kuramoto order parameter was significant in delta, theta, gamma, and the broadband condition, but not in alpha (p = 0.73) and only borderline in beta (p = 0.06). This is a TMS-perturbation result, not a resting-state predictive finding, so it is not treated as evidence that alpha is unimportant for this project's task — only as a reason not to import PLI's alpha-primacy framing into this feature family without separate justification.

**Motivating framing: baseline resting-state metastability predicting treatment response, following Escrichs et al. (2024)**

Escrichs et al. (2024) found that baseline (pre-treatment) turbulence, the standard deviation over time of the local Kuramoto order parameter, aggregated across the whole brain at each of three spatial scales, rather than computed per individual node, predicted SSRI responder status above chance (ROC-AUC 0.70 ± 0.078, p = 0.02) after 8 weeks of treatment, alongside two related measures (information cascade and information transfer) in the same classifier. Separately, at the node level, non-responders showed significantly lower metastability than both responders and healthy controls across most of 1,000 brain regions tested, a group-difference finding, not part of the predictive model. The whole-brain turbulence measure, rather than the node-level one, is the closer precedent to this project's task shape: a single aggregated baseline metastability-type value predicting treatment response in MDD.

The precedent comes with a substantial modality gap, stated explicitly rather than glossed over: Escrichs et al. use fMRI/BOLD dynamics at a 1000-region spatial resolution and SSRI response, not EEG at 26 channels and rTMS response. Bapat et al. (2024) is EEG, which is closer in modality, but its task doesn't transfer: it measures the *change* in metastability following a single TMS pulse, and this project's pre-treatment resting recordings contain no pulse for that change to be measured relative to. Bapat et al. is therefore used only as a source for computation mechanics (band-filtering, Hilbert transform, order parameter, standard deviation), not as evidence for why baseline metastability should predict treatment response — that predictive rationale comes from Escrichs et al. alone, despite its modality mismatch.

**Computation mechanics, adapted from Bapat et al. (2024):** band-pass filter to each of the five bands, extract instantaneous phase via Hilbert transform, compute the Kuramoto order parameter at each timepoint across all 26 channels, and take the standard deviation of the order parameter across all timepoints within an epoch as that epoch's metastability value. Bapat et al.'s sliding-window (50 ms) and pulse-relative baseline structure is not adopted, since both exist to track change relative to a stimulation event this project's data does not contain; metastability here is a single per-epoch summary value, aggregated across retained epochs by the same mean-aggregation approach used for band power, PLI, coherence, and PLV (Decision 3).

## 7. Age confound handling

Deferred to the modelling stage, not built into feature extraction. Age is regressed out of features within training folds only, per this project's existing documented plan, avoiding train/test leakage. Feature extraction computes raw features; age-adjustment is a fold-scoped transform applied afterward.
